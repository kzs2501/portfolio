When 40,000 people arrive for the same tickets at once, something decides who goes
first. Without a waiting room, the server decides by accident: whoever's request lands
while stock lasts. Everyone else fails at random, retries and makes it worse. A waiting
room turns that into a line. I added one to [admit-one](/projects/admit-one/), and most
of the work was making it honest.

## A place should only ever improve

The page shows three things: how many people are ahead of you, a bar that fills as the
line moves, and an estimated wait. Each one is a promise.

- **People ahead** only goes down, so the bar only ever moves forward. A progress bar
  that slips back is worse than none.
- **The wait is said coarsely:** "less than a minute", "about 3 minutes". An estimate
  is an estimate. A precise number that turns out wrong costs more trust than an honest
  range.

## Compute the place; don't count it down

It's tempting to decrement a counter on a timer. Instead, a place is a pure function of
when you joined, the clock and the line's pace:

```ts
const batchesSoFar = Math.floor((now - joinedAt) / batchInterval);
const aheadCount = Math.max(0, aheadAtJoin - batchesSoFar * admittedPerBatch);
```

Nothing ticks, so nothing drifts. A refresh resumes exactly where you were, and reading
your place never changes it. Joining twice returns the place you already have, so a
double click can't send you to the back.

## Let the server set the pace

Forty thousand browsers asking "is it my turn?" every second is a flash sale of its
own. So each answer says when to ask again: every couple of seconds near a batch, less
often otherwise. The page asks exactly that often, and not at all while the tab is in
the background.

## When it's your turn, don't grab the wheel

The obvious move is to redirect people to the tickets the moment they're admitted. But
the buyer may be in another tab, or halfway through reading something, and a page that
moves on its own is disorienting (WCAG 3.2.5). So the page:

- announces "It's your turn" and moves focus to it;
- changes the tab's title, so a tab in the background shows it;
- offers a button, and shows how long your turn lasts.

Screen readers hear the milestones too: halfway, nearly there, your turn. They don't
hear every update.

## Then limit everyone, politely

A line doesn't help if one client can hammer the reservation endpoint. So reserving is
rate-limited, and a refusal is a `429` with a `Retry-After` header saying exactly how
long to wait. The page counts down and tries again only then. Refused attempts count
toward the limit too, so hammering only lengthens the wait, while waiting as long as
you're told always works.

> A line is only fair if everyone can see it moving, and it only ever moves forward.
