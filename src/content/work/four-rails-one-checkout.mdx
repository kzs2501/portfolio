## The problem

Benefi lets people give to charities in more ways than most checkouts ever handle: a card
or Apple Pay, crypto from an exchange or a self-custody wallet on EVM chains or Solana,
shares held at a brokerage, or a grant from a donor-advised fund. Each of those **payment
rails** has a completely different sequence of steps. A card needs card capture and a
choice between one-off and monthly. A stock gift needs a brokerage, an asset, a share
count and a signed Letter of Authorization. Crypto needs a source and a network.

Built rail by rail, every new way to give meant a new flow with its own navigation,
progress, error handling and edge cases — and every shared improvement had to be made
four times. Adding a rail was a refactor.

## Context

A web team of around five engineers, a production donor app on Next.js and React, and a
product that kept adding ways to give. I owned the payments surface: the checkout itself,
guest checkout, recurring giving, and the saved-payment-method screens around it.

## My role

I architected the checkout and led the refactor that turned it from parallel per-rail
flows into a single config-driven flow, then built most of the stages on top of it.

## The key decision: a registry, not a flow per rail

Instead of encoding each rail's journey as its own sequence, every stage lives in **one
registry**, and each stage declares *when it applies*. The flow a user sees is simply the
registry filtered by their context — which rail they chose, whether they're a guest,
whether the gift recurs. Navigation, progress, back-and-forward, and error handling are
written once, against the filtered list, and never know which rail they're serving.

Three properties fell out of that structure, and each one was a product requirement:

- **Deep links.** The current stage lives in the URL, so a refresh, a shared link or the
  browser's back button lands exactly where the user was. A checkout that loses your place
  on refresh loses donations.
- **Lazy stages with prefetch.** Each stage is its own code-split chunk, and the *next*
  stage is prefetched while the user is on the current one. The flow has 30-plus stages
  across all rails; nobody downloads 30 stages to give by card, and nobody waits on a
  spinner between steps either.
- **Keeping your place.** When the context changes mid-flow — say a donor switches from
  card to stock — the flow re-resolves and the user stays on the same stage if it still
  applies, or lands at the same position if it doesn't. They never lose progress they can
  keep.

## Other frontend decisions

**Guest checkout first.** Donors can give before they have an account, and an account is
offered afterwards. I also removed a one-time-passcode step from post-donation signup.
That friction sat at exactly the moment someone had just given money, which is the worst
moment to ask for more.

**Shared flow state, decoupled stages.** Flow state lives in a store that stages read
from and write to, so a stage never imports another stage. That's what makes the
registry composable: a stage can be added, removed, or reused across rails without
anything else changing.

**Async rails get defensive states.** Crypto and stock gifts don't settle in a request:
transfers confirm later, and a Letter of Authorization has a lifecycle of its own. Those
stages have explicit processing, expired and failed states instead of a single spinner,
and the rail-specific failure modes have their own branches rather than falling into a
generic error.

**Third-party SDKs load on first mount.** The payments SDK was being downloaded on every
page. It now loads only when a payment component actually mounts. (I wrote this one up:
[Load third-party SDKs when they mount](/notes/load-sdks-on-mount).)

## Tradeoffs

**A plain registry instead of a state-machine library.** A formal statechart would give
us transition validation for free. But the flow is mostly linear with conditional stages,
and a registry of predicates is something every engineer on the team could read and
extend on day one, with no new dependency to learn. The cost is that invalid sequences
aren't impossible by construction, so tests carry that weight instead.

**Prefetch the next stage, not all of them.** Prefetching everything would make every
transition instant but would cost a donor on a phone data they'll never use. One stage
ahead is enough to hide the latency of the next step.

**One generic flow means fewer escape hatches.** When a rail needs something genuinely
unusual, it has to fit the stage contract. That constraint was the point — but it took
some negotiation with product about which differences were real.

## Quality

The fiat, crypto and stock flows each have their own test suites, covering the happy
paths and the defensive branches, written against the registry rather than against the
screens. Because the stages are data, a test can assert "a stock gift by a guest goes
through these stages in this order" without rendering a single page.

## Result

Adding a payment rail became a configuration change instead of a refactor. By our
estimate, integrating a new rail went from about three weeks to under one, and checkout
completion rose by roughly 18% after the refactor and the guest-checkout changes —
estimates rather than controlled experiments, but the direction was consistent.

## What I'd do next

Move stage configuration to the server, so product can reorder or gate stages without a
deploy, and add a small internal "flow inspector" that renders the resolved stage list
for any context. That would make the registry as legible to product managers as it is to
engineers.
